Compound interest: a game changer for retirees

Compound interest doesn't stop when you retire. Keeping part of your savings invested can help your money last longer and reduce the risk of outliving it.

How compound interest works in retirement

Younger investors are in the "accumulation phase". Retirees shift to the preservation phase, where the goal is to keep earning returns while protecting capital.

Even in retirement, your investments can keep compounding if you:

  • Keep a portion of your portfolio in dividend shares, ETFs or managed funds.
  • Reinvest dividends and interest you don't need, instead of withdrawing all your returns.
  • Use term deposits and bonds that pay compound interest.

Example: compounding after retirement

Say you retire with $500,000 and withdraw $20,000 a year. With no growth at all, the money would run out in 25 years.

Keep it invested at 5% a year instead and, because you're withdrawing less than it earns, the balance keeps growing rather than running down. In practice inflation, fees and market ups and downs make things less smooth, which is why many retirees plan around a conservative withdrawal rate.

How to use compound interest in retirement

  • Superannuation and pension funds: many retirees leave money in super so it can keep compounding in a tax-advantaged account.
  • Dividend shares and ETFs: investments that pay dividends can provide income while the rest keeps growing.
  • Avoid high-interest debt: credit card debt compounds against you and erodes savings.

Final thoughts

Even in retirement, compound interest can stretch your savings further. Keeping money invested sensibly and withdrawing at a sustainable rate can help you enjoy retirement with less financial stress.
