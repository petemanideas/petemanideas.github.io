Compound interest examples

Compound interest means earning returns on your original money and on the returns it has already earned. These eight examples show what that looks like in practice.

Example 1: $1,000 left alone for 30 years

$1,000 invested at 7% a year, compounded annually, grows to about $7,612 after 30 years. You never add another dollar — over $6,600 of that is growth on growth.

Example 2: $5,000 left alone for 30 years

The same 7% on $5,000 becomes about $38,061. Five times the starting amount gives five times the result — compounding scales with whatever you put in.

Example 3: $500 a month for 25 years

Investing $500 every month for 25 years means putting in $150,000 of your own money. Depending on the return (compounded monthly), you could end up with:

Annual return Value after 25 years
5%~$297,800
7%~$405,000
10%~$663,400

Example 4: how the snowball speeds up

$10,000 at 7% a year roughly doubles about every 10 years — and each doubling adds more dollars than the last:

  • After 10 years: ~$19,700
  • After 20 years: ~$38,700
  • After 30 years: ~$76,100
  • After 40 years: ~$149,700

The first decade adds under $10,000. The fourth adds over $73,000. That's why time matters more than almost anything else. See the rule of 72 for a quick way to estimate doubling time.

Example 5: starting at 25 vs starting at 35

Two people each invest $300 a month at 7% until age 65:

  • Starting at 25 (40 years): about $787,400
  • Starting at 35 (30 years): about $366,000

The early starter contributes only $36,000 more, but ends up with more than twice as much. More on this in compound interest for young investors and starting late.

Example 6: simple vs compound interest

$10,000 at 5% for 20 years:

  • Simple interest (5% of the original $10,000 each year): $20,000
  • Compound interest (5% of the growing balance, compounded annually): ~$26,500

Same rate, same time — compounding adds an extra $6,500 simply by earning interest on interest.

Example 7: does compounding frequency matter?

$10,000 at 5% for 10 years, compounded:

  • Annually: ~$16,290
  • Monthly: ~$16,470
  • Daily: ~$16,490

More frequent compounding helps, but only a little. The rate you earn and how long you stay invested matter far more.

Example 8: the cost of a 1% fee

Start with $10,000, add $500 a month for 30 years, compounded monthly. Earning 7% gives about $691,200. If fees cut that to 6%, you'd have about $562,500 — roughly $128,700 less. Fees compound against you just like returns compound for you.

FAQ

What is the compound interest formula?

A = P(1 + r/n)nt, where P is the starting amount, r the annual rate, n the number of compounding periods per year and t the number of years. See how compound interest works for a walkthrough.

Are these returns guaranteed?

No. Investment returns vary from year to year, and these examples use steady rates for illustration. Savings accounts and term deposits pay more predictable interest but usually at lower rates.

Do these examples include inflation or tax?

No. They show nominal amounts before tax and inflation. To think in today's dollars, subtract an inflation estimate (for example 2–3%) from the return.
